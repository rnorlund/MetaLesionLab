New here?

Three ideas in 30 seconds

  1. 1

    Words can get stuck

    After a stroke, many people still know exactly what they mean, but the word won’t come out, or comes out wrong. This is called Aphasia: difficulty finding, saying or understanding words, most often after a stroke. It affects language, not intelligence..

  2. 2

    Scientists learn from damage

    When one small area of the brain is damaged (a Lesion: an area of damage, for example where a stroke cut off the blood supply to part of the brain.), the mistakes a person makes, like “dog” for cat, hint at what that area did. But we can’t choose where the damage is, or look inside.

  3. 3

    AI has parts too

    An AI model passes each picture through a stack of Layer: one step in the stack an AI model passes information through. Muse Glimmer has 52 for language.. We can switch some parts off, safely and reversibly, and see what it loses: a Virtual lesion: switching off or disturbing chosen parts of an AI model on purpose, then giving it the same test. Nothing is permanent; switch them back on and the model is unchanged.. A Word lens: a read-out of the model’s best guess for the word after each layer, so you can see when the right word appears and where it gets lost. Researchers call it a logit lens. shows its best guess after every layer.

What’s new

The tip-of-the-tongue test, for people and AI, in any language.

We can’t look inside a brain while it searches for a word, but we can look inside an AI. Tippa Lingua gives Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open model, the same picture-naming test people take, scores it the same way, and then opens it up:

  • Damage on purpose. Switch off chosen parts (neurons, attention units, whole layers, or the part that sees) and give the test again.
  • Compare with random damage of the same size, so an effect means more than “less model”.
  • Watch the word form. The word lens shows the best guess after each of the 52 layers.
  • Set results against people. Error patterns are compared with published groups of people with aphasia, using cited, group-level numbers only.

Measured With layers 41–48 disturbed, Glimmer still named 16 of 20 pictures, but for the umbrella it said only “Umb”: the start of the word, without the rest.

New experiment · Plato’s Chair

Ask Meta’s image model for 16 chairs, and you get one chair.

Plato said there is one ideal chair. Muse Image drew the same slat-back chair 16 times; 16 people drew 16 different chairs. Its chairs are 63% less varied than people’s, and it drew 34 of 35 things more alike. Another image model, SDXL, didn’t, so this is a way to measure any image model. We also looked inside Muse Glimmer: the things people draw in more ways are harder for it to name.

Fill the gaps with Test Studio

Good picture-naming tests exist for only a few languages. Test Studio turns a word list into a checked picture set: for a language without one, for a person’s own everyday things, or for an extra version of a test.

  • Muse Image draws each picture in one consistent style.
  • SAM 3.1 checks that each picture shows one clear object.
  • Muse Spark and Muse Glimmer check that people would call it by the intended name.
  • Export printable cards, a norms table and the whole set.

Build picture tests in any language; picture sets checked in six (English, Spanish, French, Portuguese, Hindi, Mandarin). Automatic spoken scoring is verified in English, Spanish, French and Brazilian Portuguese, works with a reviewer in Mandarin, and is experimental in Hindi and other languages. How we measured

  • Muse Spark named the Spanish sierra picture serrucho, the everyday word for a hand saw.
  • A harp was named वीणा (veena) in Hindi: a reminder that picture items are cultural.

What already exists, and what’s new

Already exists

  • Good picture-naming tests. The Philadelphia Naming Test is a free download, and MultiPic offers 750 normed drawings in six European languages. Most other languages have far less.
  • Automatic error scoring, as research. ParAlg (Casilio et al., 2023) codes picture-naming errors automatically. In everyday practice, spoken answers are still mostly scored by hand, which is slow.

New here

  • One test for people and AI, scored the same way, so their errors can be compared directly.
  • A look inside. We can’t look inside a brain mid-word, but we can look inside an open AI model, layer by layer, with damage we choose and can undo.
  • End to end in several languages: make the pictures, give the test by voice, write down and score the answers, with a person checking.

Responsible use

Measurements, not diagnosis

Tippa Lingua reports accuracy, error types and speed: measurements only, with no diagnosis, no prognosis and no medical advice.

A person decides

Every automatic code can be changed by a reviewer, and exports keep both the automatic and the final code.

No patient data stored

Recordings are sent to be written down and aren’t kept; results stay in your browser until you export them. Comparisons with people use published group averages only.

Open source

Apache 2.0 code, documented scoring rules, and a citation for every comparison with people.

Second rater for scored answers, judge of related meanings, picture-name checks, and the agent that plans experiments.
Muse Voice Transcribe
Writes down spoken answers, in the test’s language.
Muse Image
Draws the test pictures in Test Studio.
SAM 3.1
Checks that each picture shows one clear object.

Every AI step uses a Meta model. The rest is plain, inspectable code: WordNet, the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary and wordfreq.
