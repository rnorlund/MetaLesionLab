How we know
  1. Muse Image, Meta’s image AI, drew 12 to 16 doodles of each of 35 everyday things, from the same request each time.
  2. Quick, Draw!, Google’s drawing game, gave us 200 people’s drawings of each thing, made in 20 seconds.
  3. DINOv2, a Meta AI that compares pictures, measured how different any two drawings look (we call it spread).
  4. Muse Spark, Meta’s chat AI, listed kinds of each thing, and Muse Image drew one of each. Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open AI model, sorted 10,020 drawings into those kinds: 83% of people’s recognizable drawings were the plain, ordinary kind, the same share as Muse Image’s.
  5. Stable Diffusion XL, an open image AI from Stability AI, got the same request: its drawings were more varied for 34 of 35 things.

“You would imagine that they had intelligence, but if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer.”

Socrates, on written words. Plato, Phaedrus 275d

Plato, the first AI skeptic, warned that writing gives “the show of wisdom without the reality” (Phaedrus 275a–b). We put his questions to AI, and to people.

What Plato said: his example was a bed

In Republic X, Socrates describes three beds: one “existing in nature, which is made by God”, one that is “the work of the carpenter”, and a third, “the work of the painter” (597b).

An image AI is Plato’s painter. It copies how a chair looks, from pictures that are copies of copies.

What Plato said about the painter

The painter copies how the carpenter’s bed looks from where he stands: “you may look at a bed from different points of view, obliquely or directly or from any other point of view, and the bed will appear different, but there is no difference in reality” (598a). Asked for a chair, Muse Image paints one look of a chair: the same chair with a slatted back, from the same angle, 16 times.

Inside Meta’s open AI model, Muse Image’s drawings of a thing merge into one

Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open AI model, reads a picture in 52 stages (layers). By the last, Muse Image’s doodles of a thing look like one picture (spread 0.09); people’s stay apart (0.54).

How we know

We showed both sets of doodles to Muse Glimmer, which anyone can download and look inside, and measured how different they look to it after each stage. People’s stayed further apart for all 35 things. The more different ways people draw a thing, the harder Glimmer finds it to name (a moderate link: −0.50 on a scale from −1 to 1, over 35 things). It named 95% of Muse Image’s doodles correctly, but only 66% of people’s.
