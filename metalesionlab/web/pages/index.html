How do we come to know a thing? Socrates and Aristotle

What we found

Meta’s image AI draws almost everything the same way. Another image AI doesn’t.

34 of 35

things Muse Image drew more alike than people do

We asked Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, for 35 everyday things, from apples to umbrellas. For a typical thing, its drawings were about half as varied as people’s (0.49 of people’s variety). Only its books were as varied as people’s.

1.30×

as varied as people’s drawings, once we named the kinds to draw

We asked Muse Image for “a chair (any kind of chair)”. It drew the same slatted chair again. Then Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, listed 16 kinds (a rocking chair, a beanbag, a throne…) and Muse Image drew one of each. Across 35 things, those drawings were 1.30 times as varied as people’s. The AI knows the variety. It just doesn’t show it unless you ask.

83%

of people’s drawings show the plain, ordinary kind, just like the AI’s

People don’t draw many different kinds of things either. Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer, sorted every drawing by kind. Across the 35 things, 83% of people’s recognizable drawings were the plain, ordinary kind: the same share as Muse Image’s. People differ in how they draw a thing far more than in which kind they draw.

2.4×

as varied: another image AI, given the same request

The sameness is Muse Image’s habit, not every AI’s. Given the same request, Stable Diffusion XL, an open image AI from Stability AI, drew pictures about 2.4 times as varied, and more varied for 34 of 35 things. Much of that extra variety is drawing style (shading, frames, scenes), not different objects.

  1. Muse Image, Meta’s image AI, drew 12 to 16 doodles of each thing, from the same request each time.
  2. Quick, Draw!, Google’s drawing game, gave us 200 people’s drawings of each thing, made in 20 seconds.
  3. DINOv2, a Meta AI that compares pictures, measured how different any two drawings look.
  4. Muse Spark, Meta’s chat AI, listed kinds of each thing. Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open AI model, sorted 10,020 drawings into those kinds.

Plato, the first AI skeptic

“You would imagine that they had intelligence, but if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer.”

Socrates, on written words. Plato, Phaedrus 275d

Plato warned that writing would give its readers “the show of wisdom without the reality” (Phaedrus 275a–b). Ask Meta’s image AI for a chair and you get one unvarying chair. So we put Plato’s questions to AI, and to people.

What Plato said: his example was a bed

In Republic X, Socrates describes three beds: one “existing in nature, which is made by God”, one that is “the work of the carpenter”, and a third, “the work of the painter” (597b). The painter copies how the carpenter’s bed looks from where he stands: “you may look at a bed from different points of view, obliquely or directly or from any other point of view, and the bed will appear different, but there is no difference in reality” (598a).

An image AI is Plato’s painter. It copies how things look, not what they are. It learned from pictures, which are copies of copies. Asked for a chair, Muse Image paints one look of a chair: the same chair with a slatted back, from the same angle, 16 times.

Quotations: Benjamin Jowett’s translation (public domain), as published by Project Gutenberg. Numbers are Stephanus pages.

Inside the model

Inside Meta’s open AI model, Muse Image’s drawings of a thing merge into one

We showed both sets of doodles to Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open AI model, which anyone can download and look inside. It reads a picture in 52 stages (layers), one after another. By the last stages, where it picks a name, it treats all of Muse Image’s doodles of a thing as nearly the same picture (spread 0.09). People’s drawings stay far apart (0.54), for all 35 things.

The more different ways people draw a thing, the harder Glimmer finds it to name (a moderate link: −0.50 on a scale from −1 to 1, over 35 things). It named 95% of Muse Image’s doodles correctly, but only 66% of people’s.
