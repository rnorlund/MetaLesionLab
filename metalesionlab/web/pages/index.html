What we found

Not just chairs. And not every AI.

34 of 35

things drawn more alike than people draw them

We asked for 35 things, from apples to umbrellas. Muse Image’s drawings were typically about half as varied as people’s (median ratio 0.49). Only books came out as varied.

1.30×

“Any kind” changes nothing. Naming the kinds does.

Ask for “a chair (any kind of chair)” and you get the same slat-back chair again. Have Meta’s Muse Spark list 16 kinds and draw one of each, and the spread rises to 1.30 times people’s. The narrow chair is a default: Muse knows the variety.

83%

People mostly draw the plain chair too

Across the 35 things, 83% of people’s recognizable drawings show the plain, ordinary kind, the same share as Muse Image’s usual prompt. People vary in how they draw a thing far more than in which one they draw.

2.4×

It’s Muse, not all AI

From the same prompt, Stable Diffusion XL’s drawings were about 2.4 times as varied as Muse Image’s, and more varied for 34 of 35 things. Much of that extra variety is drawing style, not different objects.

  1. Muse Image drew 12–16 doodles of each thing, one prompt each time.
  2. Quick, Draw! gave 200 people’s 20-second drawings of the same words.
  3. DINOv2, Meta’s vision model, measured how different two drawings look.
  4. Muse Spark listed kinds; Muse Glimmer labeled which kind each of 10,020 drawings shows.

Plato, the first AI skeptic

“You would imagine that they had intelligence, but if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer.”

Socrates, on written words. Plato, Phaedrus 275d

Plato warned that writing would give its readers “the show of wisdom without the reality” (Phaedrus 275a–b). Ask Meta’s image model for a chair and you get one unvarying chair. So we put Plato’s questions to AI, and to people.

What Plato said: his example was a bed

In Republic X, Socrates describes three beds: one “existing in nature, which is made by God”, one that is “the work of the carpenter”, and a third, “the work of the painter” (597b). The painter copies how the carpenter’s bed looks from where he stands: “you may look at a bed from different points of view, obliquely or directly or from any other point of view, and the bed will appear different, but there is no difference in reality” (598a).

An image model is Plato’s painter. It learned from pictures, copies of copies. Asked for a chair, Muse Image paints one appearance: the same slat-back chair, from the same three-quarter angle, 16 times.

Quotations: Benjamin Jowett’s translation (public domain), as published by Project Gutenberg. Numbers are Stephanus pages.

Inside the model

Inside Meta’s open model, the chairs become one point

We showed both sets of doodles to Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open-weight model, and measured their spread after each of its 52 layers. By the naming layers at the end, Glimmer files Muse Image’s doodles of a thing in essentially one place (average spread 0.09), while people’s stay spread out (0.54); people’s are more spread out for 35 of 35 things.

The things people draw in more different ways are harder for it to name: rank correlation −0.50 over 35 things. It named 95% of Muse Image’s doodles and 66% of people’s.
