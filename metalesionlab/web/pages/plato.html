How do you measure “varied”?

A Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2) turns each picture into a list of numbers that describes how it looks. Pictures that look alike get similar numbers. A set’s spread is how different two of its pictures are, on average: 16 copies of one picture would score 0. We compare Muse Image’s spread with the spread of the same number of people’s drawings.

A philosophical framing, not a finding

Aristotle’s reply: we learn what a chair is from many chairs

Plato put the Form first: one perfect, ideal chair, and every real chair a copy of it. His student Aristotle answered that the form is in the things themselves, and that we come to know it from many of them. That is roughly how an image AI learns: from millions of pictures, never from one ideal chair. It may also be why it settles on the average: the plain, textbook chair.

The two gestures Raphael gave them in The School of Athens: Plato points up, to the Forms; Aristotle holds his palm to the earth, to the things themselves. Drawn by Meta’s Muse Image.

Pretend to be 16 people

Telling the AI that a different person was drawing each time didn’t change its chair

Each time we ask Muse Image for a drawing, it starts fresh. So its 16 chairs are like 16 strangers who share one mind. Real people differ. So we told it who was drawing: a different age each time, from 20 to 80, and nothing else.

Telling it the age of the person drawing changed nothing. The chairs stayed the same, just as when we asked for “a chair (any kind of chair)”.

Over the 10 things, the variety didn’t change on average (+0.00, probably between −0.02 and +0.03). Younger or older ages didn’t move the drawings away from the usual one either. The chairs stayed the textbook chair with a slatted back, and God stayed a bearded man with a halo, 16 times out of 16. So asking Muse Image 16 times is a fair test: telling it 16 different people are drawing gives the same drawing. What does bring out its variety is naming the kinds to draw: a rocking chair, a beanbag, a throne.

We wrote down our predictions before making any pictures: 176 new doodles of the drawing pad’s 10 things and God, one request each. The one-line answer we fixed in advance for this outcome: A stated age changes nothing, like “any kind of chair”. Plan · Results

Six languages

In six languages, the AI drew the same chair, but not the same hat

We typed the same request to Muse Image in English, Spanish, Hindi, Mandarin, Arabic and Swahili: 10 doodles per language for each of 10 things, 600 in all. Is the ideal chair the same everywhere?

One word, two meanings. Swahili ndege means both “bird” and “airplane”, and 1 of the 10 Swahili “birds” was an airplane.

Inside another Meta AI

Inside Meta’s open AI model, Muse Image’s doodles of a thing merge into one

We showed the same doodles, Muse Image’s and people’s, to Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open AI model, which anyone can download and look inside (it also takes the naming test in our Model Labsister project’s Model Lab). It reads a picture in 52 stages (layers), one after another, and we read what it does with the doodles at each stage.

Method and caveats

  • How. Muse Image drew 16 doodles for each of 10 things and 12 for 25 more, from one request: “A quick simple doodle sketch of a chair, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing”. People’s drawings come from Google’s Quick, Draw! game, where players had 20 seconds; we compared Muse Image’s set with random sets of the same size from 200 drawings per thing.
  • Style mismatch. Muse Image’s doodles are clean marker sketches; people’s are quick mouse or finger drawings, often unfinished. Part of people’s spread is skill and noise, so the ranking across things means more than the exact percentage.
  • Small numbers. 12–16 AI pictures per thing, 35 things, 10 doodles per language. For any single thing the likely range is wide; the link to naming is exploratory.
  • Stand-ins. Quick, Draw! has no “boat”, “lamp” or “person”, so we used sailboat, floor lamp and yoga (Muse Image’s request was “a person doing yoga”).
  • Two image AIs. Stable Diffusion XL got the same request, but it adds shading, frames and scenes that make its pictures look more different. So its comparison with people is an upper bound: at most that varied, probably less.
  • One request, one measuring AI. Another wording moves Muse Image’s spread a lot (asking for named kinds, for example). DINOv2 measures how pictures look, not what they mean; for which kind of object a picture shows, we asked Muse Glimmer, and checked it on pictures whose kind we knew.

The full data folder (pictures, DINOv2’s number lists for them, and raw results) lives outside this repository. The app ships only statistics and the figures on this page. Read more: 35 things, six languages, inside Glimmer.
