How do you measure “varied”?

Meta’s DINOv2 vision model turns each picture into a list of numbers; similar pictures get similar numbers. A set’s spread is the average difference between two of its pictures: 0 would be 16 copies of one picture. We compare Muse Image’s spread with the spread of the same number of people’s drawings.

A philosophical framing, not a finding

Aristotle’s reply

Plato put the Form first: one ideal chair, and every real chair a copy of it. His student Aristotle answered that the form is in the things themselves, and that we come to know it from many of them. That is roughly how an image model learns: from millions of pictures, never from one ideal chair. It may also be why it settles on the average, the textbook chair.

The two gestures Raphael gave them in The School of Athens: Plato points up, to the Forms; Aristotle holds his palm to the earth, to the things themselves. Drawn by Meta’s Muse Image.

Ask it to be a crowd

What if 16 different people were drawing?

Each call to Muse Image starts fresh, so its 16 chairs are like 16 strangers who share one mind. Real people differ. So we told it who was drawing: a different age for each call, from 20 to 80, and nothing else.

A stated age changes nothing, like “any kind of chair”.

Over the 10 things, the spread changed by +0.00 on average (95% CI −0.02 to +0.03), and younger or older ages did not move the drawings away from the usual one. The chairs stayed the textbook slat-back chair, and God stayed a bearded man with a halo, 16 times of 16. So asking 16 times is fair to Muse Image: telling it that 16 different people are drawing gives the same drawing. What does bring out its variety is naming the kinds (above).

Method and caveats

  • How. Muse Image drew 16 doodles for each of 10 things and 12 for 25 more, from one prompt: “A quick simple doodle sketch of a chair, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing”. People’s drawings come from Google’s Quick, Draw! game, where players had 20 seconds; we compared Muse Image’s set with random sets of the same size from 200 drawings per thing.
  • Style mismatch. Muse Image’s doodles are clean marker sketches; people’s are quick mouse or finger drawings, often unfinished. Part of people’s spread is skill and noise, so the ranking across things means more than the exact percentage.
  • Small numbers. 12–16 AI pictures per thing, 35 things, 10 doodles per language. Single-thing intervals are wide; the link to naming is exploratory.
  • Stand-ins. Quick, Draw! has no “boat”, “lamp” or “person”, so we used sailboat, floor lamp and yoga (Muse Image’s prompt was “a person doing yoga”).
  • Two image models. Stable Diffusion XL got the same prompt, but it adds shading, frames and scenes that make its pictures look more different, so its comparison with people is an upper bound.
  • One prompt, one measuring model. Another wording moves Muse Image’s spread a lot (see “Ask for variety”). DINOv2 measures how pictures look, not what they mean; for which kind of object a picture shows, we asked Muse Glimmer, and checked it on pictures whose kind we knew.
