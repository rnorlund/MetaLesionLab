How do you measure “varied”?

A Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2) turns each picture into a list of numbers that describes how it looks. Pictures that look alike get similar numbers. A set’s spread is how different two of its pictures are, on average: 16 copies of one picture would score 0. We compare Muse Image’s spread with the spread of the same number of people’s drawings.

Telling the AI that a different person was drawing each time didn’t change its chair

Its 16 chairs are like 16 strangers who share one mind. So we gave each request a different age, from 20 to 80.

The age changed nothing: the chairs stayed the same.

How we know

Each request to Muse Image starts fresh, and we told it only the age of the person drawing. For chairs, variety with ages was ×0.7 of without, probably between 0.46 and 1.11, so no clear change. Over the 10 things, the variety didn’t change on average (+0.00, probably between −0.02 and +0.03; the bars show their average spread). Younger or older ages didn’t move the drawings away from the usual one either. The chairs stayed the textbook chair with a slatted back, and God stayed a bearded man with a halo, 16 times out of 16. So asking Muse Image 16 times is a fair test: telling it 16 different people are drawing gives the same drawing, just as when we asked for “a chair (any kind of chair)”. What does bring out its variety is naming the kinds to draw: a rocking chair, a beanbag, a throne.

We wrote down our predictions before making any pictures: 176 new doodles of the drawing pad’s 10 things and God, one request each. The one-line answer we fixed in advance for this outcome: A stated age changes nothing, like “any kind of chair”. Plan · Results

Method and caveats

  • How. Muse Image drew 16 doodles for each of 10 things and 12 for 25 more, from one request: “A quick simple doodle sketch of a chair, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing”. People’s drawings come from Google’s Quick, Draw! game, where players had 20 seconds; we compared Muse Image’s set with random sets of the same size from 200 drawings per thing.
  • Style mismatch. Muse Image’s doodles are clean marker sketches; people’s are quick mouse or finger drawings, often unfinished. Part of people’s spread is skill and noise, so the ranking across things means more than the exact percentage.
  • Small numbers. 12–16 AI pictures per thing, 35 things, 10 doodles per language. For any single thing the likely range is wide; the link to naming is exploratory.
  • Stand-ins. Quick, Draw! has no “boat”, “lamp” or “person”, so we used sailboat, floor lamp and yoga (Muse Image’s request was “a person doing yoga”).
  • Two image AIs. Stable Diffusion XL got the same request, but it adds shading, frames and scenes that make its pictures look more different. So its comparison with people is an upper bound: at most that varied, probably less.
  • One request, one measuring AI. Another wording moves Muse Image’s spread a lot (asking for named kinds, for example). DINOv2 measures how pictures look, not what they mean; for which kind of object a picture shows, we asked Muse Glimmer, and checked it on pictures whose kind we knew.

The full data folder (pictures, DINOv2’s number lists for them, and raw results) lives outside this repository. The app ships only statistics and the figures on this page. Read more: 35 things, six languages, inside Glimmer.
