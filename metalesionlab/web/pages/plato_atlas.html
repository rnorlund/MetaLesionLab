What would Plato ask an AI? Does an AI draw every thing one ideal way, or only some things? · Republic X · Plato series

The Atlas of Forms

Plato thought every everyday thing, a chair, a cup, a cat, has a Form: one perfect, ideal version, which every real one copies. Does an AI draw as if that were true? We asked Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, to doodle 135 everyday things, at least 12 times each. We set its drawings beside 200 drawings of each thing by people playing Google’s Quick, Draw! game, 20 seconds per drawing. Then a Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2) measured how different the drawings of each thing are from one another (we call this spread).

Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, draws almost every everyday thing more alike than people do: lay 12 of its drawings on top of each other and one crisp picture comes out, while 12 people’s blur into a cloud.

All the numbers

Spread: how different the drawings are from one another (0 = identical). “÷ people”: a spread divided by people’s, so 1 means just as varied as people. The likely range is a 95% confidence interval.

Why are some things drawn more alike than others? Our three guesses explain little

Before making any picture, we wrote down three things that might matter: how varied people’s drawings of the thing are, how common its name is, and whether it is natural (an animal, a fruit, a body part, the weather) or made by people. These were side questions, not the main test (exploratory): we show how uncertain each answer is, and claim none of them as proven.

You can run the same test on your own image AI

The Atlas is a ready-made test (a benchmark). Have your image AI draw any of the 135 things, at least 4 pictures each (12 is better), with the same request. Put the pictures in one folder per thing and run one script. It measures them exactly as we measured Muse Image’s, against the same 200 people’s drawings per thing, which it downloads and keeps the first time.

The request (the words we typed)

A quick simple doodle sketch of {a chair}, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.

The folder

my_model/

  teddy bear/ 0.png …

The command

git clone https://github.com/rnorlund/MetaLesionLab
cd MetaLesionLab
pip install -e ".[experiments]"
python scripts/plato_benchmark.py my_model \
  --name "My model" --out my_model_atlas.json

For each thing, you get how varied your AI’s drawings are compared with people’s, with its likely range (a 95% confidence interval); the typical value over all your things; and Muse Image’s and Stable Diffusion XL’s numbers for the same things. --list prints the 135 names. Details: docs/plato_atlas.md.

How we measured, and what it doesn’t show

  • Things. The 35 things of our first experiment, Plato’s Chair; Plato’s own examples, a bed and a table; and 98 new ones. We picked the new ones at random, within 12 kinds of thing, from the 345 things in Google’s Quick, Draw! game. We left out shapes, landmarks, imaginary things, weapons, words with two meanings and near-duplicates. We published the list, the rule and our predictions before making any new picture (pre-registration).
  • Drawings. Muse Image (muse-image-1.0) got one request (its prompt): “A quick simple doodle sketch of {a chair}, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.” It made 12 pictures per new thing (16 for some of the earlier ones), in separate batches of 4. Stable Diffusion XL, an open image AI from Stability AI, drew from the same requests on our own computer. People: the first 200 drawings of each thing that the Quick, Draw! game’s own AI recognized.
  • Spread. Every picture is cropped to its ink. DINOv2, a Meta AI that compares pictures, turns each into a list of numbers that describes how it looks (an embedding). A set’s spread is the average distance between its pictures: how different they look to DINOv2. People’s spread uses the same number of pictures as Muse Image’s. Each ratio’s likely range (95% confidence interval) comes from re-drawing both sets at random many times (bootstrap resampling).
  • The averaged pictures. Each is the pixel-by-pixel average of 12 ink-cropped drawings, with the darkest pixel set to black: Muse Image’s first 12, and 12 of the 200 people’s picked at random (from a fixed random starting point). They are illustrations; the ranking uses DINOv2’s measure. A simpler measure on the raw pixels, with no AI involved, agrees (…).
  • Habits, not limits. This is what Muse Image draws when we ask for nothing special. In our first experiment, when we named kinds of chair (“a rocking chair”, “a beanbag chair”), its chairs were about as varied as people’s (Plato’s Chair). Part of people’s extra variety is how they draw, wobbly 20-second lines, not only which version of the thing they picture.
  • Limits. One request and one drawing style. DINOv2 notices drawing style as well as what is drawn. Quick, Draw! players are not everyone, and the 20-second limit shapes their drawings. With 12 pictures per thing, a single thing’s rank is uncertain: check its likely range in the table.
