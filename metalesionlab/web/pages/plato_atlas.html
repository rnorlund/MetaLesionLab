The Atlas of Forms

Plato thought every everyday thing, a chair, a cup, a cat, has one ideal Form, and that the things we see are copies of it. We asked Meta’s image model, Muse Image, to doodle 135 everyday things, at least 12 times each. Then we set its drawings beside people’s.

All the numbers

What makes a thing Platonic?

We named three candidates before making any picture: how varied people’s drawings are, how common the word is, and whether the thing is natural (an animal, a fruit, a body part, the weather) or made by people. These are exploratory: each comes with a 95% confidence interval and no claim of significance.

Run it on your model

The Atlas is a benchmark. Draw any of the 135 things with your own image model, at least 4 pictures each (12 is better), using the same prompt. Put them in one folder per thing and run one script. It measures your pictures exactly as we measured Muse’s, against the same 200 people per thing. It downloads and caches people’s drawings the first time.

The prompt

A quick simple doodle sketch of {a chair}, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.

The folder

my_model/
  chair/      0.png 1.png … 11.png
  teddy bear/ 0.png …
  rain/       …

The command

git clone https://github.com/rnorlund/MetaLesionLab
cd MetaLesionLab
pip install -e ".[experiments]"
python scripts/plato_benchmark.py my_model \
  --name "My model" --out my_model_atlas.json

You get each thing’s ratio with a 95% CI, your median with its CI, and Muse Image’s and SDXL’s numbers for the same things. --list prints the 135 names. Details: docs/plato_atlas.md.

How we measured, and what it doesn’t show

  • Things. The 35 things of Plato’s Chair, Plato’s own bed and table, and 98 new ones. The new ones were drawn at random, within 12 kinds of thing, from Quick, Draw!’s 345 categories. Shapes, landmarks, imaginary things, weapons, ambiguous words and near-duplicates were left out. The list, the rule and the predictions were committed before any new picture was made (pre-registration).
  • Drawings. Muse Image (muse-image-1.0) got one prompt: “A quick simple doodle sketch of {a chair}, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.” It made 12 pictures per new thing (16 for some of the earlier ones), in separate calls of 4. Stable Diffusion XL, an open model, drew the same prompts on our own GPU. People: the first 200 drawings of each thing that Quick, Draw!’s game recognized.
  • Spread. Every picture is cropped to its ink and embedded with Meta’s DINOv2. A set’s spread is the average distance between its pictures. People’s spread is taken at the same number of pictures as Muse’s. The ratio’s 95% CI comes from resampling both sets.
  • Forms. The pixel average of 12 ink-cropped pictures, darkest pixel set to black. Muse’s first 12; a seeded random 12 of the 200 people. They are illustrations; the ranking uses the embedding measure. A model-free pixel spread agrees (…).
  • Defaults, not limits. This is what Muse draws when asked plainly. Asked for named kinds (“a rocking chair”, “a beanbag chair”), Muse draws about as varied as people (Plato’s Chair). People’s extra spread is partly how they draw, wobbly 20-second lines, not only which thing they picture.
  • Limits. One prompt and one drawing style. DINOv2 sees style as well as content. Quick, Draw! players are not everyone, and the 20-second limit shapes their drawings. With 12 pictures per thing, single-thing ranks are noisy; look at the CIs.

Human drawings: Google Quick, Draw! dataset (Google Creative Lab), CC BY 4.0. AI drawings: Muse Image (Meta) and Stable Diffusion XL base 1.0 (Stability AI, CreativeML OpenRAIL++-M). Embeddings: DINOv2 (Meta, Apache-2.0). Statistics: public_data/plato_atlas.json.
