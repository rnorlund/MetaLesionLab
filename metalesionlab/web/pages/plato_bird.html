Plato’s Bird

Plato thought each kind of thing has one perfect, ideal version (his Form), which every real one copies. Is there one best example of a bird, then, or a fruit, or a game? We asked people and AIs the same question for 117 kinds of thing: “Name one member of the category bird” (or fruit, or game).

People’s answers come from a published study of 20 UK adults per category (Banks & Connell, 2023). We asked Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, 50 times per category. We asked Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer (one anyone can download and look inside), 50 times too.

Pick a category and compare the answers

The philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein said some categories, like “game”, have no definition: their members only share a family likeness. The psychologist Eleanor Rosch showed that such categories still have best examples: a robin feels more like a bird than a penguin does. People’s first answer shows that pull toward the best example. Is the AI’s pull stronger?

Each bar is one answer; its length is the share of answers it got. People: the first answer each UK adult said aloud (Banks & Connell, 2023). Muse Spark: asked 50 separate times. A fair comparison needs equal numbers, so the shares use as many AI answers as there were people (13 to 20), drawn at random from its 50. We merge answers only when they name the same thing (“eggplant” = “aubergine”), ignoring capitals and plurals.

Method and caveats

  • People. The Banks & Connell (2023) study of how people name members of categories (their “category production norms”): 117 categories (67 concrete, like fruit; 50 abstract, like emotion), 20 UK adults per category, each naming aloud as many members as they could in 60 seconds. We use each person’s first answer. “People who said it at all” counts how many of the 20 named a member at any point; the typicality ratings (1 to 5) came from a separate group of raters.
  • AI. The same question for every category: “Name one member of the category bird. Answer with one word or a short name only.” Muse Spark 1.3: asked 50 separate times, at its usual settings, with minimal reasoning. Muse Glimmer 30B (open weights: anyone can download it, and we ran it on our own computer): its own odds for the first word of the answer, and 50 answers at each of three randomness settings (temperatures 0.7, 1 and 1.5).
  • Fair comparison. A short list of answers looks more varied than a long one by chance alone (20 against 50), so every share and count uses the same number of answers as people gave. We wrote down our plan, measures and predictions, and saved them in the project’s public history, before collecting any AI answers.
  • Not the same task. People named members aloud for a minute and we kept their first; the AI answered once each time we asked. People are 20 UK adults, and the AI probably learned mostly from American English (it says “soccer”, people say “football”; we merge such pairs).
  • Usual settings. The AI’s narrowness here is what it does when asked once, at its usual settings. Glimmer’s own odds are about as varied as people’s answers; how varied its answers look depends on its randomness setting (see “Is it just the AI’s randomness setting?”).
