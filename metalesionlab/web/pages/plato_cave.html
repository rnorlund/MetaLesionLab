Method and caveats

  • Pictures. The project’s 100 checked line drawings (each named correctly by Muse Spark when it was made). Outlines, silhouettes and shadows were made by code: the background is flooded from the picture’s edge, so whatever it can’t reach is the object. Holes are filled, as in the human study. Three open drawings (foot, ear, nose) were closed across their biggest opening.
  • Naming. Muse Glimmer 30B, Meta’s open-weight model, running on our own GPU, was asked “Name the object in this picture with exactly one word.” Its answers were scored with the project’s standard naming coder. Muse Spark named a random half of the pictures as a second model. Muse Spark also rated each wrong real-word answer, flagging the ones that look like the picture but mean something else (“visual” errors).
  • People. Published silhouette and outline identification rates for 260 classic drawings (Snodgrass and Vanderwart) from 356 Flemish students (Wagemans et al., 2008). Their drawings are not ours, so the comparison is by concept. Those data carry no licence, so we quote only published means and our own summary.
  • Pre-registered. Predictions were committed before any model saw a picture (plan). Not every prediction held: see the write-up.
  • Limits. One drawing per object, one prompt, one way of casting the shadow. 100 objects, so single-object results are anecdotes. Answers are capped at 8 tokens, so some descriptions are cut off.
