Name the shadow

Plato’s prisoners see only shadows on the cave wall and take them for the things themselves. We showed Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer (one anyone can download and look inside), 100 everyday objects as bare shadows, and asked it to name each one. How much of a thing survives as a shadow, and does the AI lose the same things people do?

Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer, named most of the shadows; when it failed, its favorite answer was simply the word “Shadow”.

One drawing per object, one request, one way of casting the shadow. People in a published study saw different drawings of the same objects, so that comparison is by object, not by picture.

Method and caveats

  • Pictures. Our 100 checked line drawings, drawn by Meta’s image AI, Muse Image (each named correctly by Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, when it was made). Outlines, silhouettes and shadows were made by a computer program: the background is flooded from the picture’s edge, so whatever it can’t reach is the object. Holes are filled, as in the human study. Three open drawings (foot, ear, nose) were closed across their biggest opening.
  • Naming. Muse Glimmer 30B, Meta’s open-weight model (anyone can download it), running on our own computer, was asked “Name the object in this picture with exactly one word.” Our standard scoring program marked each answer right or wrong. Muse Spark named a random half of the pictures as a second AI. Muse Spark also rated each wrong real-word answer, flagging the ones that look like the picture but mean something else (“visual” errors).
  • People. Published silhouette and outline identification rates for 260 classic drawings (Snodgrass and Vanderwart) from 356 Flemish students (Wagemans et al., 2008). Their drawings are not ours, so the comparison is by object, not by picture. Those data carry no licence, so we quote only published averages and our own summary.
  • Planned in advance. We wrote down our predictions before any AI saw a picture (pre-registered: the plan). Not every prediction held: see the write-up.
  • Limits. One drawing per object, one request, one way of casting the shadow. 100 objects, so single-object results are anecdotes. Answers are capped at 8 tokens (word pieces), so some descriptions are cut off.
