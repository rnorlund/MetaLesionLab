Would an AI pass the clock test?

Doctors use a short pen-and-paper test of memory and thinking, the MoCA. One part asks everyone the same thing: Draw a clock. Put in all the numbers and set the time to 10 past 11. We gave Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, those exact words 16 times. Then we set its clocks beside the clocks of 315 adults aged 20 to 80, from a study of aging at the University of South Carolina.

Plato wrote about Forms: the one perfect, ideal version of a thing, which every real one copies. A painting of a bed, he said, is a copy of a copy. The blurry clock on the left was drawn by no one. It is 315 people’s clocks laid on top of each other, and it shows what they have in common.

Yes, by our count: Meta’s image AI drew 10 past 11, with all 12 numbers, on 13 of its 16 clocks, and never the 10:10 of watch adverts.

The test’s three points

The AI’s 16 clocks, point by point

The test gives a clock up to three points: one for drawing the circle, one for all 12 numbers in the right order, and one for two hands showing 10 past 11, with the hour hand clearly shorter. We marked every clock ourselves first. Then Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, marked them too, with a fixed checklist we wrote for an earlier experiment.

Under each clock: the time we see, and its three points. C circle, N numbers, H hands; a crossed-out letter is a missed point. Below that, what Muse Spark saw.

The ideal clock appears from the crowd

Lay 20 people’s clocks on top of each other and you get a scribble. Lay more, and a clock that no one drew comes through, with its hands at 11 and 2. Every picture here combines at least 20 people’s clocks, so no one’s own drawing can be seen.

An AI’s clock, damaged step by step

In an earlier experiment (Damage the painter) we took Stable Diffusion XL, an open image AI from Stability AI, and damaged it on purpose, a little more at each step. We damaged either the part that reads the words or the part that paints the picture, by adding random noise to its artificial neurons or by switching some of them off. Here it was asked for “a simple clock face showing 8:45”. Under each picture is Muse Spark’s checklist mark out of 3 (circle, all 12 numbers, and the time asked), averaged over the 48 pictures at that step.

This is a comparison about an AI, not a diagnosis of anyone. We don’t match these clocks to an age: even the undamaged ones look less like people’s clocks than any age group’s do, because this AI draws ornate clocks.

Your turn

Draw the clock yourself

Draw with a mouse, a finger or a pen, or draw on paper and upload a photo. We put your clock beside the two combined clocks, people’s and the AI’s, and tell you which one it looks more like. It isn’t a test, and your drawing isn’t kept.

How we did it, and what it can’t tell you

  • The words. Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, got the test’s exact words plus one sentence about the look: It drew 16 clocks. We wrote down the words, how we would mark the clocks and what we expected before it drew the first one (our plan), so we couldn’t change them after seeing the results.
  • The three points follow the test’s own rules. We marked every AI clock ourselves, from enlarged pictures with a grid, before any AI looked at it. We counted the hour hand as “clearly shorter” if it was at most 90% as long as the minute hand; the test doesn’t give a number and neither did our plan, so we chose it as we started marking.
  • The second marker, Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, looked at each picture and answered a fixed checklist: is there a circle, how many numbers, in what order, how many hands, and where does the shorter and the longer hand point. It can’t judge “clearly shorter” itself.
  • The ages. We also gave Muse Image the same words with an age in front () and nothing else about the person. We marked these clocks with the ages hidden, then matched them back.
  • People’s clocks come from 365 drawings in the Aging Brain Cohort, a study of aging at the University of South Carolina, used with the approval of its ethics board. This site shows only pictures and numbers that combine at least 20 people: no one’s drawing, name, date or own result. The study’s own raters gave people’s clocks their points.
  • “Looks like” and “typical” come from a Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2). It turns each picture into a list of numbers that describes how it looks, and says how different two pictures look (0 means identical). Before comparing, every clock is lined up on its circle and redrawn with the same pen. This only measures overall shape. Among the adults, a clock that looked less typical did not get fewer points (), so it never says whether a clock is good or bad.
  • How varied a group is: how different its clocks are from one another, on average (we call this spread). 0 would mean identical copies.
  • “Probably between” gives a range that the true value very likely falls in. If the range for a change includes zero, the change is too close to call. A “link” runs from −1 to +1, where 0 means no link at all.
  • Not a diagnosis. An AI drawing a clock tells you nothing about any person, and nothing here is health advice. Drawing on a screen isn’t the same as pen and paper.
