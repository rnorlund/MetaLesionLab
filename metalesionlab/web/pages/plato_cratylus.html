Bouba and kiki

In the Cratylus, Socrates asks whether names fit things by nature. People all over the world call a round blob “bouba” and a spiky star “kiki”. Meta’s image model has never heard a word. We asked it to draw a shape called “bouba”, and one called “kiki”.

It draws them exactly as people would. But a new word with kiki’s sounds, “keti”, comes out round: it learned the famous words, not the sounds.

Method and caveats

  • Drawings. Muse Image got one prompt: “A simple black outline drawing of a shape called ‘bouba’. One shape only, no text.” 21 words and spellings, 12 pictures each (8 for the spelling variants), from separate calls of four. None of the 236 pictures showed any letters.
  • Words. The classic bouba, kiki, maluma and takete; eight new words crossing soft consonants (b, m, l) or hard ones (k, t, p) with rounded vowels (o, u) or unrounded ones (i, e); four neutral words (fana, hasa, nafa, safa); and no name at all.
  • Roundness. Measured three ways, by code: solidity (the shape’s area over the area of its convex hull: 1 for a blob, about 0.5 for a star); the number of sharp corners; and where the picture sits between 400 people’s circles and clouds and 600 people’s stars, zigzags and lightning bolts in Meta’s DINOv2 picture space.
  • Choices. Eight code-drawn pairs of a round blob and a spiky star, each shown with the round one on the left and on the right. Muse Glimmer (open weights, on our own GPU): the probability of “left” vs. “right”. Muse Spark: six sampled answers per word.
  • People. Published numbers from Ćwiek et al. (2022), 917 people, 25 languages, who heard the words spoken. Their data on OSF carry no licence, so nothing here is computed from them.
  • Caveats. The classic words appear in thousands of web pages about this very effect, so a model can “know” the answer by reading. People heard the words; the models only ever see letters. Twelve pictures per word; the “corners” count also counts tight round lobes. Pre-registered: plan; results: write-up.
