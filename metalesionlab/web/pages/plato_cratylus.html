Bouba and kiki

In the Cratylus, Socrates asks whether names fit things by nature. People all over the world call a round blob “bouba” and a spiky star “kiki”. Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, has never heard a spoken word: it only reads letters. We asked it to draw a shape called “bouba”, and one called “kiki”. Then we made up new words with the same sounds, such as “keti”, to see whether it follows the sounds or just knows the famous words.

Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, draws “kiki” spiky like people, but not made-up words with kiki’s sounds.

Caveats: the classic words appear on thousands of web pages about this very effect, so an AI can learn the answer by reading. People heard the words; the AIs only saw letters.

Method and caveats

  • Drawings. Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, got one request: “A simple black outline drawing of a shape called ‘bouba’. One shape only, no text.” 21 words and spellings, 12 pictures each (8 for the spelling variants), made four at a time in separate requests. None of the 236 pictures showed any letters.
  • Words. The classic bouba, kiki, maluma and takete; eight new words crossing soft consonants (b, m, l) or hard ones (k, t, p) with rounded vowels (o, u) or unrounded ones (i, e); four neutral words (fana, hasa, nafa, safa); and no name at all.
  • Roundness. Measured three ways, by a computer program: solidity (the shape’s area over the area of its convex hull, a rubber band stretched around it: 1 for a blob, about 0.5 for a star); the number of sharp corners; and where the picture sits between 400 people’s circles and clouds and 600 people’s stars, zigzags and lightning bolts, as judged by a Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2).
  • Choices. Eight pairs of a round blob and a spiky star, drawn by a computer program, each shown with the round one on the left and on the right. Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer (open weights, on our own computer): how likely it thought “left” and “right” were. Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark: six answers per word.
  • People. Published numbers from Ćwiek et al. (2022), 917 people, 25 languages, who heard the words spoken. Their raw data (on OSF, a research data site) carry no licence, so nothing here is computed from them.
  • Caveats. The classic words appear in thousands of web pages about this very effect, so a model can “know” the answer by reading. People heard the words; the models only ever see letters. Twelve pictures per word; the “corners” count also counts tight round lobes. We wrote down our predictions before we made any pictures (pre-registered): plan; results: write-up.
