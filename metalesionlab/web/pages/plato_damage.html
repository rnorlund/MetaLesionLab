What would Plato ask an AI? What happens to the Form when the mind is damaged? · our own question · Plato series

Plato’s Chair, damaged

Plato said every real chair copies one ideal Form. An AI that draws (here Stable Diffusion XL) has a part that reads the words and a part that draws. We damaged each part, step by step, and asked it for a rocking chair.

Damage the language part and it loses what it is drawing: the rocking chair becomes a room, a car, a person, never the plain chair. Damage the drawing part and it loses how: the lines dissolve, and the rocking chair survives more often. The planned split held only in part (see “The Form or the hand”).

The Form or the hand

For 8 kinds of chair × 8 seeds we measured two things at each damage level: is it still the kind we asked for (Muse Glimmer reads the picture), and is it still a clean black-on-white line drawing (Glimmer again, and simple pixel counts)? To compare fairly, the two kinds of damage are matched on how much they changed the picture.

Verdict: partly supported. The “what” half held in the pre-registered test: at equal picture change, language damage lost the asked kind faster than drawing damage did. The “how” half held for noise, but reversed for neurons switched off: there, language damage also cost more “clean line drawing” (difference −5 pts; switch to “Neurons switched off” at the top), because the prompt’s style words (“doodle, black marker, no shading”) pass through the damaged language part too.

Does it go back to Plato’s chair?

In semantic dementia, people’s drawings lose the details that make a thing special and drift toward the typical member of its category: a duck gets four legs (Bozeat and colleagues, 2003). Taking that as an analogy, not a claim about people, we predicted the same here: a damaged language part should turn the rocking chair back into the plain chair.

Damage the AI that looks, not the one that draws

Muse Glimmer, Meta’s open model, looked at Muse Image’s doodles of named kinds (a throne, a wheelchair, a top hat …) and picked which kind each one shows. Then we damaged Glimmer: the neurons most active for language, for naming, or for seeing, and the same number of random neurons as a control.

One model that draws and reads

Janus-Pro-7B (DeepSeek, open weights) draws pictures and reads them with the same language model. We switched off a share of that language model’s neurons and asked it both to draw chairs and to say which kind of thing Muse Image’s doodles show. Do the two fail together?

Clocks

Drawing a clock is an old drawing task in neurology; here it is only an analogy. We asked SDXL for “a simple clock face showing” six different times, intact and damaged, and had Muse Spark read each clock with a checklist we wrote (outline, the 12 numbers in order, two hands, the time shown).

Method and caveats

  • The drawer. Stable Diffusion XL base 1.0, on a lab GPU. Its language part is its two text encoders (CLIP ViT-L and OpenCLIP bigG); its drawing part is the UNet. Damage is reversible: hooks change the neurons’ signals on the way through, never the weights.
  • Damage. Either a share of the neurons switched off (cumulative: a higher level keeps every neuron a lower one switched off), or Gaussian noise added to every neuron, scaled to each neuron’s usual range. The same seed at every level makes the pictures pairs.
  • Matched severity. The two parts break at different doses, so they are compared at equal amounts of picture change (DINOv2 distance to the intact twin), not at equal doses.
  • Readers are models. Muse Glimmer judges the kind and the drawing style; Muse Spark reads the clocks; DINOv2 measures similarity. Each can be wrong, and a picture of “a throne” can be a fine drawing of a chair that Glimmer calls plain.
  • An analogy, not a claim about people. This is a model of how damage to parts of an AI changes its drawings. The link to semantic dementia and to clock drawing is an analogy; nothing here says anything about any person.
  • Pre-registered. Hypotheses, measures and doses were committed before the main runs, after a small pilot that set the dose range.
