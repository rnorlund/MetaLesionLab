What would Plato ask an AI? What happens to an AI’s idea of a chair when we damage it? · our own question · Plato series

Plato’s Chair, damaged

Plato said every real chair copies one perfect, ideal chair: his Form. We tested an AI that draws: Stable Diffusion XL, an open image AI from Stability AI. It has a part that reads the words (its text encoders) and a part that paints the picture. We damaged the AI on purpose, one part at a time and a step at a time: we added random noise to its artificial neurons, or switched some of them off. Then we asked it for a rocking chair.

Damage the words part and the rocking chair becomes a room, a car or a person, never a plain chair. Damage the drawing part and the lines dissolve, but the rocking chair survives more often. We predicted this split before the experiment; it held only in part (see Does it forget what to draw, or how?).

Damage the part of a drawing AI that reads the words and it forgets what it is drawing (the rocking chair becomes a car); damage the part that paints and it forgets how, but keeps the chair more often.

Our planned prediction held only in part: see Does it forget what to draw, or how? This is an analogy, not a claim about people.

Does it forget what to draw, or how?

We asked for 8 kinds of chair, each from 8 random starting points, and checked two things at every damage step. Is it still the kind we asked for? (Muse Glimmer looks at the picture and says.) And is it still a clean black-on-white line drawing? (Glimmer again, plus simple pixel counts.) The two parts break at different rates, so we compare them when the damage has changed the picture by the same amount.

Verdict: partly supported. We predicted damage to the words part would make the AI forget what to draw, and damage to the drawing part how to draw. The “what” half held in our planned main test: at equal picture change, words damage lost the asked kind faster than drawing damage did. The “how” half held for added noise, but reversed for neurons switched off. There, words damage also cost more clean line drawings (difference −5 pts; switch to “Neurons switched off” at the top), because our request’s style words (“doodle, black marker, no shading”) pass through the damaged words part too.

Does a damaged AI fall back on its plain, ordinary chair?

In semantic dementia, a brain disease that slowly wears away what words and things mean, people’s drawings lose the details that make a thing special. They drift toward the typical member of its kind: a duck gets four legs (Bozeat and colleagues, 2003). As an analogy, not a claim about people, we predicted the same here: damage to the words part should turn the rocking chair back into the plain chair the AI draws when asked for just “a chair”.

What if we damage an AI that looks, instead of one that draws?

Muse Glimmer looked at quick drawings by Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, of named kinds (a throne, a wheelchair, a top hat …). For each one, it picked which kind it shows from a list. Then we damaged Glimmer: we switched off (set to their average) the neurons most active for language, for naming, or for seeing. For a fair comparison, we also switched off the same number of neurons picked at random.

In an AI that both draws and reads, does damage take both skills?

Janus-Pro, an open AI from DeepSeek that both reads and draws pictures, uses one language core for both jobs. We switched off a share of that core’s neurons. Then we asked it to draw chairs, and to say which kind of thing Muse Image’s drawings show. Do the two skills fail together?

On a damaged AI’s clocks, the numbers go first

Drawing a clock is an old test that neurologists (brain doctors) give patients; here it is only an analogy. We asked Stable Diffusion XL for “a simple clock face showing” six different times, undamaged and damaged. Then Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, checked each clock against a list we wrote: an outline, the 12 numbers in order, two hands, the time shown.

Method and caveats

  • The drawer. Stable Diffusion XL base 1.0, run on a lab computer. The part that reads the words is its two text encoders (CLIP ViT-L and OpenCLIP bigG); the part that paints the picture is its UNet. The damage is temporary: small add-ons (hooks) change the neurons’ signals as they pass through, and never touch the AI’s stored settings (its weights).
  • Damage. Either a share of the neurons switched off (a heavier step keeps every neuron a lighter step switched off, and adds more), or random noise (Gaussian) added to every neuron, scaled to each neuron’s usual range. The same random starting point (seed) at every step makes the pictures matched pairs.
  • Matched severity. The two parts break at different amounts of damage, so we compare them at equal amounts of picture change, not equal damage. Picture change is how different the damaged picture looks from its undamaged twin to a Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2).
  • The judges are AIs. Muse Glimmer judges the kind and the drawing style; Muse Spark reads the clocks; DINOv2 measures how alike pictures look. Each can be wrong, and a picture of “a throne” can be a fine drawing of a chair that Glimmer calls plain.
  • An analogy, not a claim about people. This is a model of how damage to parts of an AI changes its drawings. The link to semantic dementia and to clock drawing is an analogy; nothing here says anything about any person.
  • Planned in advance (pre-registered). We wrote down our predictions, measures and damage amounts, and saved them publicly, before the main runs. A small trial run beforehand set the range of damage.
