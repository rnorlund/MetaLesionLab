Plato’s Chair · the Platonic clock

What time does an AI draw?

We asked Meta’s image model, Muse Image, for a quick doodle of a clock, 16 times, without saying what time. Then we looked at 200 people’s 20-second drawings of a clock.

…

10:10 is the time on almost every watch and clock advert: the hands frame the brand name and look like a smile. Plato said a painter copies a carpenter’s bed, and so makes a copy of a copy. Muse Image’s clock looks like a copy of a photo of a product.

The average clock

The hands above were read by another AI. As a check that needs no reading at all, we averaged the pixels of each set of doodles. If the clocks agree, the average shows sharp hands.

Plato’s own examples: the bed and the table

Plato’s famous example is not a chair. In Republic X he writes of beds and tables: one Form of the bed, made by a god; the bed a carpenter makes by looking to it; and a painter’s picture of that bed, a copy of a copy. We ran both through the same test as the chair.

How we measured, and caveats

  • Pictures. Muse Image drew 16 clock doodles with no time (“A quick simple doodle sketch of a clock, black marker on plain white…”), 8 for each of 12 asked times (“…of a simple clock face showing 3:00…”), and realistic photos (“A photo of a wall clock showing 3:00.”) for 6 times. Stable Diffusion XL, an open image model, got the same prompts. People’s clocks are the first 200 recognised “clock” drawings in Google’s Quick, Draw! game, where players had 20 seconds.
  • Reading the time. Meta’s Muse Spark read every clock’s hands. We first tested it on clocks drawn by code with known times: … We also read a random sample by eye without looking at its answers: … Most disagreements were the hour, one off, on Muse Image’s doodles, whose hour hand often sits between two numbers: the reader called five of its 10:10 V’s 11:10. So for the clocks with no time asked we also give counts by eye, and the pixel average needs no reading at all.
  • About 10:10 means within 10 minutes of 10:10, with the hour and minute hands either way round. If drawn times were random, 5.7% would count.
  • Pre-registered. The plan, with its predictions, was written before any picture was drawn (plan).
  • Defaults, not flaws. These are what the models draw when the prompt leaves things open. Muse Image draws a requested time when asked.
  • Bed and table. The same pipeline as Plato’s Chair: 16 Muse Image doodles each, compared with random sets of 16 from 200 people’s drawings, with Meta’s DINOv2 vision model measuring how alike the pictures look. The 35-thing results are unchanged.
