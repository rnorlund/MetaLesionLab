What time does an AI draw?

We asked Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, for a quick doodle of a clock, 16 times, without saying what time. Then we looked at 200 clocks drawn by people playing Google’s Quick, Draw! game, 20 seconds per drawing.

10:10 is the time on almost every watch and clock advert: the hands frame the brand name and look like a smile. Plato said a painter copies the bed a carpenter made, so a painting is a copy of a copy. Muse Image’s clock looks like a copy of a product photo.

Ask Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, for a clock without naming a time, and it draws 10:10, the time on watch adverts; people almost never do.

No AI reading involved

Stack the clocks, and Muse Image’s average shows one sharp 10:10

The hands above were read by another AI, Muse Spark. As a check that needs no reading at all, we averaged the pixels of each set of doodles, as if stacking them on top of each other. If the clocks agree, the average shows sharp hands. Average Muse Image’s 16 clocks and one sharp 10:10 comes out; people’s 200 blur into a ring.

Plato, Republic X

Plato’s own examples: Muse Image draws beds and tables more alike than people do

Plato’s famous example is not a chair. In Republic X he writes of beds and tables: one Form of the bed (the one perfect, ideal bed), made by a god; the bed a carpenter makes by looking to it; and a painter’s picture of that bed, a copy of a copy. We ran both through the same test as the chair: how different Muse Image’s drawings are from one another, compared with people’s.

How we measured, and caveats

  • Pictures. Muse Image drew 16 clock doodles with no time (“A quick simple doodle sketch of a clock, black marker on plain white…”), 8 for each of 12 asked times (“…of a simple clock face showing 3:00…”), and realistic photos (“A photo of a wall clock showing 3:00.”) for 6 times. Stable Diffusion XL, an open image AI from Stability AI, got the same requests. People’s clocks are the first 200 recognised “clock” drawings in Google’s Quick, Draw! game, where players had 20 seconds.
  • Reading the time. Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, read every clock’s hands. We first tested it on clocks drawn by code with known times: … We also read a random sample by eye without looking at its answers: … Most disagreements were the hour, one off, on Muse Image’s doodles, whose hour hand often sits between two numbers: the reader called five of its 10:10 V’s 11:10. So for the clocks with no time asked we also give counts by eye, and the pixel average needs no reading at all.
  • About 10:10 means within 10 minutes of 10:10, with the hour and minute hands either way round. If people drew times at random, 5.7% would count.
  • Planned in advance. We wrote down the plan and our predictions before we made any pictures (pre-registered: the plan).
  • Habits, not flaws. These are what the AIs draw when the request leaves things open. Muse Image draws a requested time when asked.
  • Bed and table. The same method as the main Plato’s Chair experiment: 16 Muse Image doodles each, compared with random sets of 16 from 200 people’s drawings. A Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2) measured how alike they look. The results for the 35 everyday things were not rerun and are unchanged.
