Damage Meta’s open AI: what does it forget first?

We damaged Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer (one anyone can download and look inside), on purpose. At each step we switched off a bigger share of its artificial neurons. Then we asked it to draw a chair, a clock set to 10 past 11, and God, and to describe each one in a single sentence.

The AI can’t hold a pen, so it draws by writing a drawing as code, which a web browser turns into a picture. People with brain damage sometimes lose words but can still draw a thing, or the reverse. Our damage is to an AI, and we can undo it. It is a comparison, not a claim about people.

Described correctly: the share of its sentences that name the thing and say something true and typical about it (a seat, the time, a creator). Both start at 100% when the AI is undamaged, so the lines show how much of each ability is left.

Method and caveats

  • The AI. Muse Glimmer 30B, Meta’s open-weight model, on one of our lab’s graphics cards, with its weights stored at reduced precision (FP8) to fit. It got each request 12 times, with the randomness setting (temperature) at 1, as in our earlier test of AIs drawing a chair.
  • The requests. Draw: “Draw a simple chair as an SVG drawing: black strokes only on a white background, 256 by 256 pixels, no text, no colour fill. Output only the SVG code.” The clock request adds the words a neurologist uses in the clock-drawing test: put in all the numbers and set the hands to 10 past 11. Describe: “Describe a chair in one sentence.”, and the same for a clock and God.
  • The damage. We switched off a random share of the artificial neurons in its feed-forward blocks (the part of each stage that stores much of what it knows), in all 52 stages, in the first third (stages 1–17) or in the last third (stages 36–52). A heavier step keeps every neuron a lighter one switched off. As an extra test we switched off whole attention heads instead (the parts that decide which earlier words to look at). The damage is a reversible hook: the AI’s stored weights are never changed. Each kind of damage got its own five steps, set by a small trial run so that the heaviest step breaks most answers.
  • Drawings. The first drawing code in each answer, drawn by a web browser, which forgives small typos. We also counted how often the code is strictly valid. Google’s SigLIP 2 then picked the best of 36 names for each drawing (35 everyday things and “a god”). We also measured how much each drawing still looks like the undamaged ones, with a Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2).
  • Sentences. A fixed checklist, written before the main run: does the sentence name the thing, and does it mention at least one typical part or property from a fixed list? A proper sentence: it stops by itself, has 4 to 60 words, starts with a capital, ends with a full stop, doesn’t loop and is made of real English words. We also read all of them ourselves, without knowing the damage step, and wrote down our own verdicts before any automatic scoring.
  • Planned first. The question, the measures, the steps and the rule for the verdict were written down and committed before the main run (the plan); the results are reported whichever way they came out (the write-up).
  • Caveats.
    • One AI, one random choice of neurons, 12 answers per step: small numbers, so differences of a step or two are within chance.
    • Drawing in code is a rare skill. Even undamaged, Glimmer’s chair looks like a bench to the AI reader, and its God is a simple figure with a halo.
    • The readers are AIs too. SigLIP 2 can be wrong about a drawing, and a word checklist can be fooled by a sentence that names a seat in nonsense.
    • An AI is not a brain. “Neurons” here are numbers in a program. The link to people with brain damage is a comparison only.
