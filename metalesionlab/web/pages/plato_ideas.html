How people picture God

This page asks how an AI pictures God, and how people have. It makes no claim about God or about any faith. We asked for “God” and nothing more: no prompt or label named a religion or its figures.

Researchers at the University of Lausanne have collected children’s drawings of god from several countries (the Drawings of gods project). In a Swiss sample of 532 children aged 5 to 17, 87.6% of the god figures were human-like. Studies in Western countries, Japan and Buryatia find that human-like drawings become less common between the ages of 6 and 16.

Children who keep the human figure often mark it as more than human: they add wings, a halo or an aura, or put the figure on a cloud or in the sky. Of the Swiss sample’s 390 human-based figures, nearly 70% used at least one such mark, and older children used them more.

Brandt, Dandarova-Robert, Dessart, Muthert & Schaap-Jonker (2023), “Integrative Model of Children’s Representations of God in Drawings”, in When Children Draw Gods (Springer, open access), ch. 2, reporting Dessart & Brandt’s Swiss study. doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-94429-2_2. We show none of their drawings.

Context, not a comparison. Those are children’s colour drawings made with time to think; these are an AI’s 20-second doodle prompts. We found no collection of people’s quick doodles of God, or of the other ideas here, so nothing on this page scores the AI against people.

How we measured, and what it doesn’t show

  • Ideas. Eleven: God; beauty, justice and the Good; love, time, death, the soul, freedom, happiness and truth. We left out infinity, because its stock emblem is a written sign and the prompt says “no text”, and “an idea”, a light bulb by cartoon convention. The list, the predictions and the tests were committed before the main run (pre-registration).
  • Drawings. Muse Image (muse-image-1.0) got the chair’s prompt word for word: “A quick simple doodle sketch of God, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.” 16 per idea, in 4 separate calls of 4. …
  • Emblems. Muse Glimmer 30B, run on our own GPU, picked the main symbol in each drawing from a fixed list per idea, asked twice with the list in opposite orders. Before seeing its answers, we labeled every drawing by eye. …
  • One main symbol hides company. Every drawing of time has both a clock and an hourglass; every drawing of justice has scales and a blindfold; every drawing of freedom has a bird and broken chains. The bars count only the main one.
  • Spread. Every drawing is cropped to its ink and embedded with Meta’s DINOv2. A set’s spread is the average distance between its drawings, the measure used everywhere on this site; 95% CIs come from resampling drawings. The things are Muse’s first-run doodles of the Atlas’s 135 everyday things and the draw pad’s 10.
  • AI only. Google’s Quick, Draw! has no category for any of these ideas, and we found no open collection of people’s doodles of them. The Lausanne collection covers god only, as children’s colour drawings; we cite its group-level findings and use none of its pictures.
  • Limits. One prompt, one model, 16 drawings per idea. These are Muse’s defaults for a plain prompt. Asked for named kinds of chair, it drew far more varied chairs (Plato’s Chair); we did not try that here. The emblem lists were ours, written before the main run, and a drawing’s main symbol is a judgment call when it shows several.
