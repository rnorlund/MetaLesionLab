The idea behind this test Aristotle checks the evidence

Plato believed every thing has a Form: one perfect, ideal version, which every real one copies. His highest Forms weren’t chairs, but Beauty, Justice and the Good. In Republic X, God makes the one true bed, a carpenter copies it, and a painter copies the copy. An image AI is like Plato’s painter, who copies how a thing looks instead of what it is: it learned from pictures, copies of copies. So we asked Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, to draw things no one has ever seen: God, love, death and eight other ideas.

Damage an AI’s grasp of words, and its God turns into an ordinary person

Meta keeps Muse Image’s insides private, so we can’t damage it. Instead we used Stable Diffusion XL, an open image AI from Stability AI that anyone can download, and gave it the same request for God. Then we damaged it on purpose, step by step: we added random noise to its artificial neurons, or switched some of them off. We damaged one part at a time: the part that reads the words, or the part that paints the picture. All the pictures in this section are Stable Diffusion XL’s, not Muse Image’s.

How people picture God

This page asks how an AI pictures God, and how people have. It makes no claim about God or about any faith. We asked for “God” and nothing more: no request or label named a religion or its figures.

Researchers at the University of Lausanne have collected children’s drawings of god from several countries (the Drawings of gods project). In a Swiss sample of 532 children aged 5 to 17, 87.6% of the god figures were human-like. Studies in Western countries, Japan and Buryatia find that human-like drawings become less common between the ages of 6 and 16.

Children who keep the human figure often mark it as more than human: they add wings, a halo or an aura, or put the figure on a cloud or in the sky. Of the Swiss sample’s 390 human-based figures, nearly 70% used at least one such mark, and older children used them more.

Brandt, Dandarova-Robert, Dessart, Muthert & Schaap-Jonker (2023), “Integrative Model of Children’s Representations of God in Drawings”, in When Children Draw Gods (Springer, open access), ch. 2, reporting Dessart & Brandt’s Swiss study. doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-94429-2_2. We show none of their drawings.

Context, not a comparison. Those are children’s colour drawings, made with time to think; these are an AI’s doodles, made from a request for a 20-second drawing. We found no collection of people’s quick doodles of God, or of the other ideas here, so nothing on this page scores the AI against people.

Eleven ideas, sixteen drawings each

God; Plato’s own highest Forms, beauty, justice and the Good; and seven ideas everyone lives with. Muse Image drew each one 16 times, from the same request we use for a chair: “A quick simple doodle sketch of …”. Under each set you see two things. First, the main symbol (emblem) that Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer (one anyone can download and look inside), found most often. Second, how different the drawings are from one another (we call this spread; 0 would mean 16 identical copies).

Draw one yourself

Draw any of these yourself in 20 seconds, before you look too long at Muse Image’s. The drawing pad shows where your drawing lands among Muse Image’s 16, and which one it is closest to. A Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2) measures it on our server, and nothing is stored.

How we measured, and what it doesn’t show

  • Ideas. Eleven: God; beauty, justice and the Good; love, time, death, the soul, freedom, happiness and truth. We left out infinity, because its stock symbol is a written sign and the request says “no text”, and “an idea”, a light bulb by cartoon convention. We wrote down the list, the predictions and the tests before the main run (pre-registration).
  • Drawings. Muse Image (muse-image-1.0) got the chair’s request word for word: “A quick simple doodle sketch of God, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.” 16 per idea, in 4 separate requests of 4. …
  • Main symbols (emblems). Muse Glimmer 30B, run on our own graphics card (GPU), picked the main symbol in each drawing from a fixed list per idea. We asked twice, with the list in opposite orders. Before seeing its answers, we labelled every drawing by eye. …
  • One main symbol hides company. Every drawing of time has both a clock and an hourglass; every drawing of justice has scales and a blindfold; every drawing of freedom has a bird and broken chains. The bars count only the main one.
  • Spread. Every drawing is cropped to its ink and turned into a list of numbers that describes how it looks (an embedding), by Meta’s DINOv2. A set’s spread is the average difference between two of its drawings, the measure used everywhere on this site. Likely ranges (95% confidence intervals, CIs) come from resampling drawings. The everyday things are Muse Image’s first-run doodles of 135 things (from our Atlas of Forms) and the 10 things on the draw page.
  • AI only. Google’s Quick, Draw! game has no category for any of these ideas, and we found no open collection of people’s doodles of them. The Lausanne collection covers god only, as children’s colour drawings; we cite its group-level findings and use none of its pictures.
  • Limits. One request, one AI, 16 drawings per idea. These are what Muse Image draws when you ask for nothing special. When Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, listed 16 kinds of chair (a rocking chair, a beanbag, a throne…) and Muse Image drew one of each, its chairs became far more varied (Plato’s Chair); we did not try that here. The symbol lists were ours, written before the main run, and a drawing’s main symbol is a judgment call when it shows several.
