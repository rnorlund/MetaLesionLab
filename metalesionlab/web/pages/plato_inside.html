Inside the model

Plato thought every real chair copies one perfect, ideal chair. Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, draws nearly the same chair every time, while people playing Google’s Quick, Draw! game, 20 seconds per drawing, draw many different ones (the main experiment). We showed both sets to Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer (one anyone can download and look inside), and asked it to name each drawing. Glimmer reads a picture in 52 stages (layers), one after another, and we looked at what it holds after each one. First it sees a drawing; stage by stage, it settles on a name.

    Method and caveats

    • Pictures. 35 things from Plato’s Chair: every drawing by Meta’s image AI, Muse Image (16 for 10 things, 12 for 25), and the same number of drawings by people playing Google’s Quick, Draw! game: 920 pictures in all, each padded to a white square 448 pixels wide.
    • What Glimmer holds. Muse Glimmer 30B, Meta’s open-weight model (anyone can download it), ran on our own computer. It read each picture with the request “Name the object in this picture with exactly one word.” After each of its 52 layers (stages) we kept its state: the 6,656 numbers that describe the picture at the spot where it writes its answer.
    • Spread: how far apart the drawings are. The average cosine distance (a standard measure of how different two lists of numbers are) between two drawings of a thing from the same source, after centring the numbers over all 920 pictures. 0 would mean all in one spot. The main Plato’s Chair experiment uses the same measure, and the chart uses all 6,656 numbers.
    • “Filed in one place” is a line we drew. It is the first stage from which Muse Image’s drawings stay less than half as spread out as people’s, all the way to stage 52 (we called it the collapse layer). We chose the 0.5 after seeing the data, not before.
    • The word lens reads names, not ideas. It applies Glimmer’s last, word-choosing step to each stage’s numbers, to see which word it is heading towards (a logit lens). The stage where it first reads the name shows when the name becomes readable there, not when the idea of a chair forms. Early stages aren’t tuned to that last step, so the lens says little about them.
    • The answer spot. In the middle stages, the spot where Glimmer will write its answer holds little about which thing a drawing shows, so spread there is mostly not about the thing. In the last stages it partly is the answer itself.
    • Drawing style. Muse Image’s drawings are clean marker sketches; people’s are 20-second mouse or finger drawings, often unfinished. Part of people’s spread is skill and noise.
    • Person. Quick, Draw! has no “person”, so both sides drew people doing yoga; Glimmer often answers with pose names (“Tree pose”, “Meditation”).
    • Not planned in advance (exploratory). 35 things, 12–16 drawings per source, one model, one request, one picture size. We didn’t write down predictions before running it (nothing here was pre-registered).
