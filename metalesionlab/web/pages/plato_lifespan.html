The life of a chair

In Plato’s Meno, Socrates argues that learning is really remembering the Forms: the one perfect, ideal version of each thing, which every real one copies. If he is right, a child growing up and an AI being trained should both move toward the same chair. We followed children’s drawings from age 2 to 10, and an open image AI, Stable Diffusion, through five stages of its training.

Children’s chairs grow more like adults’ with age; Stable Diffusion, an image AI, never gets closer to people’s.

Averages over 20 everyday things. Children drew with a fingertip at a museum kiosk, adults in a 20-second online game, and the AI makes polished pictures. So most of the AI’s distance from people’s drawings is drawing style.

Watch an AI learn what a chair is

The first public version of Stable Diffusion, an open image AI, can already draw a chair, which means it can’t show us an AI’s first steps. Instead, we trained our own small AI from nothing: a model that learns to draw from people’s sketches (Sketch-RNN). It learned from 203,324 chairs drawn by people playing Google’s Quick, Draw! game, 20 seconds per drawing. We trained it four separate times. This is the larger of two sizes we built; the smaller one is described below.

Here its first training run draws the same 16 chairs at every stage of training. Each chair always starts from the same random starting point; only what the AI has learned changes. Its chairs can only be as detailed as the quick sketches it learned from.

Method and caveats

  • Children. 15,820 drawings of 20 things by children aged 2 to 10, drawn with a fingertip on a tablet at a museum kiosk in 30 seconds each (Long, Fan, Huey, Chai & Frank, 2024). Every age and thing has at least 23 drawings. We rendered their strokes exactly like the adults’ drawings. We never show a child’s drawing. The data are licensed non-commercially with share-alike, and they are children’s, so this page shows group-level numbers only.
  • Adults. 200 recognized drawings per thing from Google’s Quick, Draw! game (20 seconds, mouse or finger). Their average drawing of each thing is our “adult Form”, a stand-in for Plato’s ideal.
  • AI. Muse Image: chair doodles from Meta’s image AI, made for the main Plato’s Chair experiment. Stable Diffusion, an open image AI: five public versions saved during its training (checkpoints v1.1 to v1.5). Each got the same request and the same 16 random starting points (seeds) for 21 things: 1,680 pictures, made on our own graphics card (GPU). Versions 1.3, 1.4 and 1.5 all branch from 1.2, and each version was trained on different pictures as well as for longer.
  • Sketch models. Small AIs that draw with pen strokes, in the style of Sketch-RNN (Ha & Eck, 2017), trained from a random start (random weights) on our own graphics cards. Their drawings are rendered exactly like people’s.
    • The one in the animation has 2,048 units (its artificial neurons). It learned from all 203,324 recognized Quick, Draw! chairs (not the 200 adult reference chairs) for 102,400 training steps, 4 times with different random starting points (seeds).
    • A first, smaller one (512 units) learned from 20,000 of those chairs for 25,600 steps, 8 times. As a check, it also learned from 20,000 cats, 5 times. Our measure recognized its chairs, but they looked rough to the eye, which is why we trained the larger one.
    • At every stage of training, each run drew 200 drawings at each of several randomness settings (sampling temperatures), from random starts that never change. A lower setting means more cautious drawing.
  • Measures, all made with a Meta AI that compares pictures (DINOv2):
    • Recognizable: how often a drawing is closest to the adults’ average drawing of its own thing, out of 21 things.
    • Distance from the adults’ average (the “adult Form”): how different a drawing looks from it (cosine distance; 0 = identical).
    • Variety: how different drawings of the same thing are from one another, always compared in sets of 16.
    Likely ranges (95% confidence intervals, CIs) come from resampling drawings and things (bootstrap).
  • Planned first. We wrote down the plan and our predictions before measuring any child’s drawing or making any AI picture (the plan). The small sketch model’s eight runs had their own plan, written down before training: the test, the seeds, the randomness settings and which drawings the animation shows (the sketch plan). The larger model’s test was added to that plan after its first run and before the other three were trained. It uses only those three.
  • Caveats.
    • The conditions differ: a fingertip at a kiosk, a 20-second online game, and generated pictures that look like marker sketches. The AI’s distance from people’s drawings is largely drawing style.
    • Ages are whole years entered by a parent, and parents sometimes helped.
    • Children at a science museum are not a sample of all children.
    • Stable Diffusion’s public versions start well into its training. The sketch AIs show the earliest stages, but they learn only from adults’ drawings, and they are two sizes of one design, so they show what learning from people’s drawings can look like, not what children do.
    • The picture-comparing AI (DINOv2) measures how drawings look, not what anyone knows.
  • The end of the lifespan is an analogy, not a claim about people: see what happens when we damage an image AI on purpose, in Damage the painter.
