What would Plato ask an AI? Does an AI’s chair change as it grows? · Our own question · Plato series

An AI growing up

Some researchers argue that as AIs grow, they come to see the world in more and more similar ways, as if settling on one shared picture of it. They call this the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh, Cheung, Wang & Isola, 2024). If they are right, do bigger AIs also draw more alike, settling on one ideal chair like Plato’s Form, the one perfect chair every real chair copies? We asked 15 open AIs, from half a billion to 33 billion parameters (the adjustable numbers inside an AI), and three of Meta’s AIs to draw a chair in SVG, a way of writing a picture as text code. And we trained one small sketching AI on 100 to 20,000 chairs drawn by people playing Google’s Quick, Draw! game, 20 seconds per drawing.

Bigger open AIs fail less often but don’t agree on one chair; Meta’s Muse Wonder always draws the same one.

Every AI got the same request: “Draw a simple chair as an SVG drawing: black strokes only on a white background, 256 by 256 pixels, no text, no colour fill. The short answer, in four parts

Bigger AI

Bigger AIs fail less, but don’t draw more alike

The smallest open AIs fail 4 to 8 times in 12: blank canvases, canvases covered in ink, broken code. From 4 billion parameters up, they rarely fail. But even at 32 billion, none of the three open families draws a chair another AI can name, and their drawings don’t get more alike: 0.93 to 1.6 times as varied as people’s.

Meta’s AIs

Meta’s Muse Wonder draws the same chair every time

Muse Wonder, an AI that writes drawing code, drew the same plain chair with a slatted back 12 times. Its chairs are 0.38 times as varied as people’s: as alike as the chairs of Meta’s image AI, Muse Image (0.37). Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, drew plain side-view chairs, about as varied as people’s (0.95). Meta doesn’t publish their sizes, so this says nothing about growing.

More training

More training makes chairs easier to name, not more alike

With more people’s chairs to learn from, the small sketching AI’s chairs become easier for an AI to name (59% → 73%). But how different they are from one another (their spread) stays about 0.31 from 100 to 20,000 chairs, always below that of the people’s chairs it learned from (0.36 to 0.37).

Do AIs agree?

AIs from different makers don’t come to draw alike

Do AIs from different makers draw more alike as they grow? Not here. Their chairs are no closer together at 30 to 33 billion parameters than at 2 billion and below. The idea being tested is about how AIs represent the world inside, and a drawing written as code may not show it.

What we predicted, and what happened

Before running anything, we wrote down three predictions: as an AI grows, its chairs would become easier for an AI to name (P1) and more alike (P2), and it would fail less (P3). One check differs: Qwen3’s chairs do become more alike at a lower randomness setting (temperature 0.7). AIs from different makers don’t come to draw alike as they grow

The idea. Four researchers (Huh, Cheung, Wang and Isola, ICML 2024) argue that as AIs grow, they come to judge how alike two things are in more and more similar ways, as if converging on one shared, “Platonic” picture of the world. If drawings showed this, big AIs from different makers would draw chairs more alike than small ones do.

The test. We group the AIs by size. Within each group, we measure how different two makers’ sets of chairs look to DINOv2, a Meta AI that compares pictures. (The measure is called energy distance: 0 means two AIs draw the same range of chairs.) Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer (one anyone can download and look inside), joins the 30–33 billion group.

The result. They don’t come together. The biggest group (30–33 billion) is no closer than the smallest (a change of +0.047, likely range −0.029 to +0.120). The smallest AIs look alike only because they all scribble. The simple distance between makers’ chairs does shrink a little with size (0.55 to 0.49), but each AI’s own chairs also become about that much more alike.

What it doesn’t show. The idea is about how AIs represent the world inside, not about drawing a chair in code, which few AIs were trained to do. And the one AI here that drew a single ideal chair, Muse Wonder, has no published size.

Method and caveats

  • The AIs. Three open families of language AIs (anyone can download them), all under the Apache-2.0 licence, free to download without signing anything, and fixed to exact versions on Hugging Face: Qwen2.5-Instruct 0.5B, 1.5B, 7B, 14B and 32B, from Alibaba (our main family); OLMo 2 Instruct 1B, 7B, 13B and 32B, from AI2, the Allen Institute for AI; and Qwen3 0.6B to 32B, from Alibaba, with its step-by-step “thinking” switched off. Plus Meta’s open AI model, Muse Glimmer 30B, and Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark 1.3, and Muse Wonder 1.0 through Meta’s online service, the Meta Model API (reasoning effort “minimal”). Not used: Qwen2.5-3B and -72B (not Apache-2.0), Llama and Gemma 3 (you must accept a licence to download them, and we never accept one on anyone’s behalf).
  • Answers. 12 answers per AI at temperature 1 (the usual randomness setting), and 12 at 0.7 (less random) as a check. The open AIs used plain temperature sampling (no top-p or top-k) and at most 2,048 new tokens (word pieces); those of 30B parameters and more ran with FP8 weights (a compressed number format) on one 46 GB graphics card.
  • Turning code into pictures. The first complete SVG in each answer, drawn at 256 × 256 pixels with no access to files or the network. A failure is no SVG, an SVG the drawing program rejects, a blank canvas, or one more than 95% ink.
  • The sketching AI. A Sketch-RNN-style model (about 1.1 million parameters) trained from scratch on nested random sets of 100 to 20,000 Google Quick, Draw! chairs (each bigger set contains the smaller ones), 3 training runs each (seeds: different random starting points), for the same 25,600 steps. A second analysis takes each run at the point where it did best on 1,000 chairs it never trained on (its lowest held-out loss). 200 drawings per run.
  • Measures. Every drawing is cropped to its ink, and Meta’s DINOv2 turns it into a list of numbers that describes how it looks (an embedding). Named correctly (recognizable): the drawing is closer to the average of people’s chairs than to the average of people’s drawings of any of 34 other things (the nearest of 35 concept centroids). Spread: the average difference between two drawings. Check: Google’s SigLIP 2, an AI that matches pictures to words, also named every drawing without examples (zero-shot; added after the plan, because DINOv2 calls a slatted chair seen from the front a ladder).
  • Planned first. We published the design, the predictions and the rule for verdicts before any run (the plan). The results are reported whichever way they came out (the write-up).
  • Caveats.
    • One request (prompt) and default settings: this is how each AI answers this request, not what it could draw with help.
    • Drawing in SVG code is a narrow skill. An AI can know chairs well and still write poor SVG.
    • Muse Spark and Muse Wonder thought briefly before answering, even at “minimal” effort: about 420 and 125 tokens (word pieces) per answer. The open AIs answered directly.
    • 12 drawings per AI and 4 to 6 sizes per family, so every trend describes these AIs (it is descriptive), not a general law.
    • Spread is measured by one picture-comparing AI, DINOv2, and it notices drawing style as well as shape.
