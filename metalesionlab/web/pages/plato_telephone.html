Plato’s Telephone

Telephone is the party game where a whispered message changes as it passes down a line. We played it with two of Meta’s AIs. Meta’s chat AI, Muse Spark, looks at a drawing of a chair and describes it in a sentence or two. Meta’s image AI, Muse Image, draws that description. Spark describes the new drawing, and so on, ten times.

Asked just for “a chair”, Muse Image nearly always draws the same plain wooden chair with a slatted back: its textbook chair. Plato thought every real chair copies one ideal chair, its Form, and called a painting a copy of a copy. Would copying pull every chair toward Muse Image’s textbook chair?

Copied ten times by two AIs, most chairs drifted from the AI’s usual chair, and their kind was forgotten first.

Only Spark’s sentence passes from one picture to the next, so anything it leaves out is lost. A small test: 8 starting chairs plus 2 things that aren’t chairs, for comparison, with 3 chains each.

Which words survive the copying?

Only Spark’s words pass from one picture to the next, so anything it doesn’t say is lost. At each copy we checked three things. Does the description still say “chair”? Does it still name the kind of chair (rocking, throne, beanbag …)? And when we show Spark the picture with a list of 16 kinds of chair, does it still pick the right kind?

Plato on copies of copies

In Plato’s Republic (Book X), Socrates ranks three beds. First the Form: the one perfect, ideal bed, which every real bed copies. Then the carpenter’s bed. Then the painter’s picture of the carpenter’s bed, which is a copy of a copy.

In another dialogue, the Parmenides, Plato turns the worry on the Forms themselves. If a thing is a likeness of its Form, that likeness seems to need yet another Form, and so on without end. A chain of copies is the practical version: every picture here is a copy of a description of a copy.

People have played this game. In 1932 the psychologist Frederic Bartlett passed drawings from person to person, each redrawing the last one (Remembering). In his best-known chain, an unclear drawing turned into an ordinary face. That chain is famous, but when Carbon and Albrecht (2012) tried it in five experiments, they found no consistent drift toward the face. Researchers have also passed made-up languages from learner to learner, each learning from the one before (this is called iterated learning). The languages became easier to learn and more regular (Kirby, Cornish & Smith, 2008). Theory and experiments suggest such chains settle on what the learners already expect (Griffiths & Kalish, 2007; Xu & Griffiths, 2010). On that view, where a chain ends up shows the copier’s own expectations.

Why it matters for AI. When new AIs are trained on text or pictures made by older AIs, they can lose the rare, unusual examples: a problem called “model collapse” (Shumailov et al., 2024, Nature). Our chains involve no training, only two finished AIs copying each other. So we ask a smaller question: are unusual chairs the rare examples that get lost?

Method and caveats

  • Starting pictures. Eight chair doodles that Muse Image drew for Plato’s Chair when asked for a particular kind of chair (rocking chair, throne, beanbag, wheelchair, office chair, folding chair, high chair, Adirondack chair). For comparison, two things that aren’t chairs (a top hat and a cuckoo clock): our controls. Three separate chains per starting picture, ten copies (generations) each: 300 new pictures.
  • Each round. Muse Spark (version muse-spark-1.3, with its thinking before answering set to the minimum) gets the current picture and these words: “Describe this drawing so someone could redraw it. One or two sentences. Don’t name an artist.” Muse Image (muse-image-1.0) gets Plato’s Chair’s usual doodle request, with Spark’s description in place of the object: “A quick simple doodle sketch of {description}, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.”
  • Measures. DINOv2, a Meta AI that compares pictures, turns each picture (cropped to its ink) into a list of numbers that describes how it looks. The difference between two pictures is the cosine distance between their lists (0 = identical). The textbook chair, as measured, is the average of the 16 doodles Muse Image drew for plain “a chair”. Spark also labeled each picture’s kind of chair from a list of 16 kinds (or 12 kinds of hat, and of clock). The likely ranges (95% confidence intervals) come from bootstrapping: repeating the analysis on many random re-draws of the 8 starting chairs, then of the chains within each.
  • Planned first. We wrote down the design and our predictions before running any chain (the plan). We report each result however it came out (the write-up).
  • Caveats.
    • Only Spark’s sentence passes from one picture to the next: what it doesn’t say can’t survive, and what it stresses gets drawn bigger. So this measures the pair, describer and drawer, not Muse Image alone.
    • One way of asking for a description, one way of asking for a drawing, one thinking setting. Shorter descriptions, or a different drawing AI, could behave differently.
    • Turning a picture into words always loses something, so no chain is expected to stay identical.
    • Eight starting chairs and two comparison pictures are few. Each comparison is a single picture.
    • This is how these two Muse models behave with their usual settings in one set-up, not a general property of Meta’s AIs.
