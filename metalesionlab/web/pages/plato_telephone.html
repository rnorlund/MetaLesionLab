Copies of copies

In Republic X, Socrates ranks three beds: the Form, the carpenter’s bed, and the painter’s picture of the carpenter’s bed, a copy of a copy.

In the Parmenides, Plato turns the worry on the Forms themselves: if a thing is a likeness of its Form, the likeness seems to need yet another Form, and so on without end. A telephone chain is the practical version: every picture here is a copy of a description of a copy.

People have played this game. Frederic Bartlett (Remembering, 1932) passed drawings from person to person, each redrawing the last. In his best-known chain an ambiguous drawing turned into a conventional face. That chain is famous, but Carbon and Albrecht (2012) ran five experiments and did not find a systematic drift toward the face. In iterated learning, each learner learns from the previous learner’s output. Made-up languages passed along such chains became easier to learn and more structured (Kirby, Cornish & Smith, 2008), and theory and experiments suggest that chains settle on what the learners already expect, their prior (Griffiths & Kalish, 2007; Xu & Griffiths, 2010). On that view, where a chain ends up shows the copier’s own expectations.

Why it matters for AI. Models trained on earlier models’ output can lose the rare parts of the data, the tails of the distribution: “model collapse” (Shumailov et al., 2024, Nature). Our chains involve no training, only two models copying each other at inference time, so this asks a smaller question: are unusual chairs the tails that get lost?

Method and caveats

  • Starts. Eight of Muse Image’s own named-kind doodles from Plato’s Chair (rocking chair, throne, beanbag, wheelchair, office chair, folding chair, high chair, Adirondack chair) and two controls that aren’t chairs (a top hat and a cuckoo clock). Three independent chains per start, ten generations each: 300 new pictures.
  • Each step. Muse Spark (muse-spark-1.3, minimal reasoning) gets the current picture and: “Describe this drawing so someone could redraw it. One or two sentences. Don’t name an artist.” Muse Image (muse-image-1.0) gets Plato’s Chair’s doodle prompt with the description in place of the object: “A quick simple doodle sketch of {description}, black marker on plain white, like a 20-second drawing, no text, no shading.”
  • Measures. Meta’s DINOv2 image model compares pictures (cosine distance between their embeddings, after cropping each to its ink). Muse’s default chair is the average of the 16 doodles it drew for plain “a chair”. Spark also labeled each picture’s kind of chair from a list of 16 (or 12 kinds of hat and clock). CIs come from resampling the 8 starting chairs and then the chains within each.
  • Planned first. The design and predictions were committed before any chain was run (the plan). Results are reported whichever way they came out (the write-up).
  • Caveats.
    • Spark’s sentence is the bottleneck: what it doesn’t say can’t survive, and what it stresses gets drawn bigger. This measures the pair, describer and drawer, not Muse Image alone.
    • One describing prompt, one drawing prompt, one reasoning setting. A shorter description, or a different drawer, could behave differently.
    • Turning a picture into words always loses things, so no chain is expected to stay identical.
    • Eight starting chairs and two controls are few. The controls are one picture each.
    • This is how Muse behaves by default under one protocol, not a general property of Meta’s models.
