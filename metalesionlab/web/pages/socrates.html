The dialogue

How to talk with Socrates

He asks what a thing is. You answer in a sentence. He tests your answer against a case from the experiments, and you mend it. After three to five exchanges he admits that neither of you knows, and shows what held and what broke.

      How it works

      1. Look it up. Muse Spark, playing Socrates, calls read-only tools over results already measured: Plato’s Chair, asking for variety, inside Muse Glimmer, the newer Plato experiments as they’re published, and a small set of Plato passages. At most five per turn.
      2. Answer, then ask. A short answer that cites where each number comes from, then one question back to you.
      3. Check. Every number must appear in a tool result, and every quotation of Plato must match a verified passage word for word. What fails is fixed once or left out.
      4. Or let him examine you. His first question is written in advance. Each definition you give is tested against one hard case from the experiments: a kind Muse Spark named, the AI’s textbook example against people’s, Plato’s painter, a shadow in the Cave. Two to four sentences a turn, one question each.
      5. Remember nothing. Your browser keeps the dialogue (up to eight turns; an examination, up to five) and sends it with each message. This site stores none of it.

      What is the Socratic method?

      1. Profess ignorance. Socrates says he doesn’t know, and asks you what a thing is. In the Meno (71a–b) he admits he doesn’t know what virtue is, and asks Meno to tell him.
      2. Test the definition. This is the elenchus: a case your definition lets in that doesn’t belong, or leaves out that does, sends you back to mend it.
      3. End in puzzlement. Often nobody wins. Meno compares Socrates to a torpedo fish that numbs whoever touches it (Meno 80a–b); this aporia is where looking starts.
      4. Be a midwife. In the Theaetetus (150b–151d) Socrates calls himself a midwife: he helps others bring out their ideas and tests whether they are sound, but delivers none of his own.

      These are paraphrases, not quotations. The site quotes Plato only from its verified passages of Jowett’s translation, and Aristotle, Plato’s student, only from Ross’s.
